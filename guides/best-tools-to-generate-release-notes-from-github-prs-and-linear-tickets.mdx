Release notes are the most automatable document in software: everything they describe already exists as a merged PR title, a closed Linear ticket, a commit message. And yet they ship late or not at all, because turning the internal record into something a customer should read is nobody's favorite Friday task.

Two separate jobs hide in this search: *drafting* the notes from GitHub and Linear activity, and *delivering* them to customers. The tools below split on which job they do. One of them is Modem, which we build; judge our entry with that in mind.

## 1. Modem

[Modem](https://modem.dev/) starts from the connection the other tools don't have: it already links shipped work to the customer conversations that asked for it. Feedback from [Slack](https://modem.dev/integrations/slack), [Discord](https://modem.dev/integrations/discord), [support](https://modem.dev/integrations/zendesk), and [email](https://modem.dev/integrations/email) clusters into topics; when a PR merges in a [connected repo](https://modem.dev/integrations/github) or a [Linear ticket](https://modem.dev/integrations/linear) completes, the work is matched back to the topic and the people behind it.

So the [agent](https://modem.dev/docs/features/agent) doesn't just draft release notes from the merge record: it drafts them knowing who cared, tailored notes, personalized digests, or replies posted into the conversations where the requests originally landed. That's release notes as [loop-closing](https://modem.dev/close-the-loop) rather than broadcasting: the eight customers who asked for the export fix hear that it shipped, in their channel, in terms of what they asked for.

**Where it fits:** teams that want shipped-work communication driven by who was waiting. **Where it doesn't:** if you need a public changelog page as the artifact, pair it with one of the page-first tools below.

## 2. Released

[Released](https://www.released.so/) is the Jira-native answer: it watches your boards and development activity and uses AI to draft release notes from tickets, organized into a changelog you can publish as a page or in-app. If writing the notes is the bottleneck and the source of truth is Jira, it's the most direct fit on this list.

Its center of gravity is the Atlassian ecosystem, so teams on Linear and GitHub should verify current coverage of their stack before committing.

**Where it fits:** Jira shops that want AI-drafted notes from the tickets they already write.

## 3. LaunchNotes

[LaunchNotes](https://www.launchnotes.com/), built by ex-Atlassian folks, is a product-communication platform: announcement pages, categorized updates, and subscriber notifications, so customers and internal teams can follow the streams they care about. It's aimed at orgs where release communication is a process with stakeholders, not just a page.

Drafting still leans on a human editor shaping what the dev-activity record means; the platform's strength is the publishing and subscription machinery around it.

**Where it fits:** process-heavy product orgs communicating changes to segmented audiences.

## 4. Beamer

[Beamer](https://www.getbeamer.com/) puts the notes where users already are: an in-app widget plus email, with segmentation so an announcement reaches the users it's relevant to. Pricing is MAU-based, starting around $49/month, with segmentation and reactions on higher tiers.

It's a delivery tool: the drafting from PRs and tickets is yours, and its capture of whether anyone cared runs through reactions and feedback on the announcement itself.

**Where it fits:** products where in-app visibility of "what's new" moves adoption.

## 5. GitHub's auto-generated release notes

[GitHub generates release notes natively](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/releasing-projects-on-github/automatically-generated-release-notes): one click (or API call) on a release compiles merged PR titles since the last tag, grouped by label, with first-time-contributor callouts, configurable through a `release.yml`. Free, zero setup, and it's the right default for developer-facing repositories where the audience reads GitHub.

It's a compilation of PR titles, not customer-facing prose, and it knows nothing about Linear or about which customers were waiting.

**Where it fits:** open source and API/SDK products whose users live on the Releases page.

## 6. DIY: Actions plus an LLM

The build-it option: a GitHub Action that collects merged PRs and closed Linear tickets since the last release, hands them to an LLM with your voice guidelines, and opens the draft as a PR to your changelog. Teams like [Ascend have written up the pattern](https://www.ascend.io/blog/how-we-built-an-ai-powered-release-notes-pipeline), and it's a weekend of work that fits your exact format.

Like all internal tooling, it's free until it breaks the week the person who built it is on vacation, and delivery to customers is still a separate problem.

**Where it fits:** teams with strong opinions about format and someone happy to own a pipeline.

## How to choose

Decide which half you're missing. If it's *drafting*, GitHub's native generation (developer audiences), Released (Jira), or a DIY pipeline covers it. If it's *delivery*, Beamer and LaunchNotes are publishing machinery. If it's *both, and specifically reaching the customers who asked*, that's the version [Modem](https://modem.dev/close-the-loop) automates: the merge event triggers it, the feedback graph supplies the audience, and the agent drafts the message in terms of the original request. The test: pick a feature you shipped last month and ask each tool who should be told. Only one kind of tool can answer with names.
